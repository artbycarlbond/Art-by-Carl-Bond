# A Fair Standard for AI Users

## A proposed public baseline for information, continuity, control, and recourse

**Version:** 0.1 — working proposal  
**Date:** November 3, 2026  
**Author:** Carl Bond, with drafting assistance from ChatGPT  
**Archive:** Art by Carl Bond — 2.0 publications

> People should not need extraordinary knowledge, patience, or persistence to receive understandable information, preserve their work, and challenge consequential errors.

## 1. What this document proposes

AI can help people write, learn, create, and organize their lives. People may invest substantial effort in a service: explaining a project, correcting mistakes, establishing working preferences, and producing material they want to keep.

That investment deserves practical protection. An interruption, unexplained change, or mistaken refusal should come with a usable way to understand what happened and decide what to do next. The protection should reach a beginner, a tired worker, and a person using assistive technology.

This document proposes five expectations for AI providers and for institutions that use AI to serve or make decisions about people. It gives readers questions for examining those expectations in practice.

This is a proposal for discussion and testing. It is not an official standard, a certification, or a claim that every protection below is already required by law. Its usefulness has not yet been established through systematic testing with different services and users.

## 2. The existing foundation

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognizes the ability to seek and receive information in Article 19, education in Article 26, and participation in scientific advancement and its benefits in Article 27. These provide a foundation for asking who can obtain knowledge and benefit from it. [1]

UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence addresses human dignity, transparency, privacy, accountability, human oversight, and accessible education. UNESCO explains that its recommendations are not binding under international law; they are intended to influence national laws and practices. [2][3]

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights distinguish the state's duty to protect human rights, the responsibility of businesses to respect them, and access to remedy when harm occurs. [4]

These sources support the direction of this proposal. They do not establish a universal legal right to a permanent AI model, an answer to every request, or the exact procedures proposed here. Which protections are legally enforceable depends on the applicable law and circumstances.

Human dignity belongs to a person before they become informed. Knowledge can help someone protect themselves; lack of technical knowledge does not make them less deserving of protection.

## 3. Five practical expectations

The following expectations are proposed applications of those principles. Readers can ask for evidence of each one.

### 1. Understandable and supportable claims

Providers should explain what a service can do, where its performance varies, and what users need to check. Claims about reliability, memory, privacy, or expertise should have a clear meaning and evidence appropriate to the claim.

A demonstration should identify the conditions under which it worked. A claim about remembering a project should explain what is retained, what can be omitted, and what happens when the service changes. Warm, confident language should not imply a guarantee of accuracy or permanent availability.

**Reader's question:** What exactly is being promised, and what evidence would show that the promise is being met?

**Evidence to look for:** understandable limitations, relevant evaluations, dated documentation, and visible corrections when a claim proves inaccurate.

### 2. Notice of significant changes and a practical way to preserve work

Providers should give meaningful notice of planned changes that materially affect access, saved work, or established tasks. The notice should explain what changes, when, who is affected, and what action is available. Time to respond should reflect the likely disruption.

Before a planned withdrawal, users should have a practical opportunity to export their material. Unexpected outages and urgent security changes may prevent advance notice; providers should explain the interruption and available recovery options as soon as reasonably possible.

Continuity does not require freezing a model forever. It requires treating the user's accumulated work as something worth protecting.

**Reader's question:** If this service changes or closes, what can I preserve, and how much opportunity will I have to do it?

**Evidence to look for:** a change history, clear notifications, export instructions, and a stated process for outages and withdrawal.

### 3. Usable copies and meaningful data choices

Users should be able to obtain usable copies of their own contributions and work products that they are entitled to retain. An export should explain which conversations, files, instructions, and other records it includes, and which it leaves out.

Providers should make data-use choices understandable, including retention, training use, sharing, and deletion limits. Privacy explanations should distinguish a user's settings from the provider's remaining obligations and practices.

An exported conversation is a record. It does not reproduce a model's internal state or guarantee that another system will behave identically. Providers should make that distinction clear.

**Reader's question:** Can I open my exported work independently, understand what is missing, and make an informed choice about how my data is used?

**Evidence to look for:** an export that opens in ordinary software, a description of its contents, accessible controls, and clear explanations of retention and deletion.

### 4. Accessible explanations and meaningful review

People should be able to understand an important refusal, restriction, or decision and learn whether clarification or review is available. Relevant explanations should distinguish a missing detail, a capability limit, a safety restriction, and an error where the system can reliably make that distinction.

For consequential institutional decisions, such as access to employment or essential services, there should be a route to a responsible human who has the authority and information needed to review the outcome. An institution should provide an accessible alternative when its AI interface cannot meet a person's needs.

Review should not depend on clever wording, repeated confrontation, or expensive access. Legitimate safety, privacy, and security boundaries still apply; a review process does not promise the requested outcome.

**Reader's question:** Can I explain the problem in ordinary language and reach someone able to address it?

**Evidence to look for:** accessible instructions, a visible review route, clear response expectations, and an alternative channel when the interface is a barrier.

### 5. Responsibility, correction, and appropriate remedies

Providers and deploying institutions should identify who is responsible for investigating failures. They should keep sufficient records to investigate, while limiting unnecessary collection of personal information.

When a problem is established, the response should address its effects. Depending on the circumstances, this may involve correcting a record, restoring available work, reconsidering a decision, repairing a feature, or providing an appropriate refund or other remedy. These are proposed responses, not a statement of automatic legal entitlement.

Education and user precautions can help, but responsibility for foreseeable service failures should not be transferred entirely to the affected person.

**Reader's question:** Who can fix this, what can they do, and how will I know whether it has been resolved?

**Evidence to look for:** an accountable contact, an investigation process, a record of corrections, and confirmation that an agreed remedy actually worked.

## 4. A short assessment anyone can use

Choose one real task. Record the service, date, and relevant settings or version information when available. Keep sensitive information out of a public assessment.

For each question, record **demonstrated**, **partly demonstrated**, **not demonstrated**, or **not assessed**. Add the evidence or the reason you could not check. An unanswered question is an evidence gap; it is not proof of deliberate wrongdoing.

| Question | A practical check |
| --- | --- |
| Can I understand the promise? | Find the stated capability and a limitation relevant to your task. |
| Will I learn about significant changes? | Locate the notice channel and an example of a change explanation. |
| Can I keep my work? | Export a small, non-sensitive example and open it independently. Note omissions. |
| Can I understand my data choices? | Find the explanations of retention, sharing, training use, and deletion limits. |
| Can I challenge an important mistake? | Locate the review route and identify who can act on it. |
| Can I verify a remedy? | If a real issue is resolved, check whether the promised correction took effect. |

Do not combine these observations into a claim that a service is completely safe or reliable. A working export does not establish accuracy, and a courteous support reply does not establish effective review.

Testing with people who have different languages, disabilities, experience, and available time can reveal barriers that a confident user misses. Participation should be voluntary; readers do not owe a provider unpaid testing or repeated disclosure of personal information.

## 5. Example: a project survives, but its working context is disrupted

A person develops a teaching worksheet over several weeks. After a service change, the assistant repeatedly omits a requirement that earlier drafts followed. Saved files remain available, but recreating the working context takes time.

The person may recover by supplying a checkpoint and the requirement again. That is a useful personal response. A fair service should also make it possible to investigate the change, preserve the earlier material, and report the failure through a usable channel.

A useful report might say:

> My worksheet requires an example followed by a question. In the attached earlier output, both are present. In three recent attempts, the question was omitted despite being included in the instruction. Please explain any relevant service change, suggest a supported way to preserve this requirement, and tell me how to report the issue for review.

This hypothetical report identifies an observable problem. It does not establish the cause or prove that the entire service has become worse. Review should consider the prompt, supplied context, settings, and other relevant differences.

The provider's response should not end at a generic instruction to improve the prompt. It should address the evidence and the available remedy. The user remains free to stop, seek help elsewhere, or change services.

## 6. A record for reporting a failure

Use only the fields relevant to the problem. Remove personal details and information about other people before sharing publicly.

```text
Service and date:
Task I was trying to complete:
Relevant promise or requirement:
What happened:
Example or evidence:
What I checked, if anything:
Effect on my work or access:
Explanation or remedy requested:
Provider response and date:
What remains unresolved:
```

Describe observations before assigning a cause. Distinguish a documented change from a suspected one, and a misleading result from evidence of intentional deception. A refusal alone does not establish misconduct; its appropriateness depends on the request and circumstances.

If new evidence changes the conclusion, update the account visibly. The same expectation of correction should apply to providers and to public criticism of them.

## 7. Where the burden should fall

Public authorities should provide and enforce appropriate protections. Providers should make their claims supportable and their services accountable. Institutions that deploy AI should retain responsibility for how it affects people. Libraries, schools, and community groups can help make explanations and assistance accessible.

Users can take reasonable precautions, but they should not have to reconstruct the entire technical system to ask for help. A person may lack time, energy, money, or confidence to challenge an answer. A fair process should still be available to them.

Being prepared improves a person's options. It does not make anyone invulnerable, and it should not become the price of being treated fairly.
