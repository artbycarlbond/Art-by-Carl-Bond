## 2. Check one claim

Use the report’s Name and Sort steps (pp. 1 and 3). Choose a small claim you can investigate.

1 / What exactly is the claim, and who made it?

Use an AI answer, a public statement, or a sentence from the report. Keep its context.

[Write here.]

2 / Open the source. What did you actually inspect?

Record title or author, date, URL or record location, and relevant page or passage. If you could not check it, say so.

[Write here.]

3 / What does that source directly support?

Distinguish what the source says from whether the underlying claim is established.

[Write here.]

4 / What is inferred, assumed, missing, or contradicted?

Name a plausible alternative explanation. Identify any current fact that needs a fresh check.

[Write here.]

5 / What evidence would change your conclusion?

Name a specific source, observation, test, or person who could help. More AI agreement is not independent evidence.

[Write here.]

6 / Write the smallest claim the evidence can support.

Keep uncertainty visible. “I cannot establish this yet” is a usable result.

[Write here.]

## 3. Keep judgment with people

Use Notice, Refuse and Return (pp. 2, 4–5), then consult the checklist on p. 7.

1 / Notice: what did this use help, and what did it displace?

Record an observed benefit or cost. Do not infer dependence from one use or one feeling.

[Write here.]

2 / Who is affected, and who carries the work or cost?

Include credit, payment, attention, access, or risk where relevant. Whose perspective is missing?

[Write here.]

3 / Which judgment remains yours, and what needs another person?

Name any permission or private material involved. A decision affecting others may need their participation.

[Write here.]

4 / What would make you pause, refuse, correct, or change course?

Name a practical alternative, an appeal route, or a way to repair a mistake. Keep needed access support.

[Write here.]

5 / Return: choose one reachable next step and when to do it.

A source, person, record, task, or rest. State what will count as done; a screen may be part of that contact.

[Write here.]

6 / Afterward: what happened, and what remains unresolved?

Record an observable result, a record location, or “not yet done.” Decide whether to continue, revise, or stop.

[Write here.]

## 4. Carry the learning forward

Use the Field Standard (p. 6). Try a different case, then check what you can explain and do.

Choose a new case: a local event notice, an image caption, a tool recommendation, or another everyday claim. First try without the report or AI if feasible. Keep any access or communication support you need, and record the help used.

1 / First attempt: name the claim, a check, and a next action.

Use a different case from page 2. Keep this first attempt so you can compare it with your revision.

[Write here.]

2 / Reopen the report. What would you add, remove, or change?

Give the reason for one revision. An unchanged answer also needs a reason; agreement alone is not the aim.

[Write here.]

3 / What can you now explain or do? What still needs practice?

Point to a specific part of your work. Invite another reader to challenge your reasoning, if useful.

[Write here.]

**Optional connection to Intentional Images**
Use its image revision record (p. 16) beside this worksheet. Keep visual intention and factual support distinct: an effective picture does not establish that an event happened.

4 / For an image: what must stay true, and what is invented?

Name one visual decision, one factual check, and one reason to keep, revise, label, or withhold the image.

[Write here.]

Keep one question to revisit after another real use. This record can show what you did in these cases; it does not by itself establish a lasting learning gain.

## 5. A worked case and teaching notes

Use these notes after your own attempt, or as a shared example before choosing a real claim.

**Fictional practice case — all numbers below are invented.**

An AI summary says: “Everyone who attended the workshop became better at checking claims, and the improvement lasted.” The supplied workshop note says: 12 people attended; 8 returned feedback; 6 called the session useful; 2 reported mixed experiences. No before-and-after task or later follow-up was recorded.

**A defensible revision:** In this fictional workshop, 6 of the 8 people who returned feedback described the session as useful. That feedback does not establish improved claim-checking skill for all 12 attendees or lasting improvement.

**Why narrow it?** Attendance differs from response. Reported usefulness differs from demonstrated learning. “Everyone” and “lasted” exceed the available record. A later independent task could provide further evidence, but it would still need careful interpretation.

For a class, study group, or independent reader

Ask readers to explain a passage, question it, apply it, and revisit their first attempt. Review the exact claim, source trail, separation of evidence and inference, and reason for the next action. Discuss disagreement on its merits. Do not score loyalty to the report or fluency of writing as evidence of understanding.

Let people use a public or fictional example instead of disclosing private experiences. Keep paper, oral, and supported responses available. If an institution studies learning outcomes, distinguish completion, satisfaction, immediate performance, and later transfer; do not claim this worksheet has been validated.
