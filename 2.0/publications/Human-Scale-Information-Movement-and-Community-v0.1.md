# Human Scale: Information, Movement, and Community

## Keeping knowledge accessible while making room for everyday life

> A phone can put information within reach. Human scale also has to fit a human body, a day, and a community.

## 1. If it is on the phone, why go anywhere?

Sometimes a phone completes the task. A person checks opening hours, reads a document, talks with a distant friend, or gets an explanation. An unnecessary trip has been avoided. That is a useful result.

Other tasks involve materials, movement, a place, or people sharing an activity. Information can help someone prepare, but the activity still has to happen. Reading instructions for growing food and tending a garden are different parts of the work.

The useful question is: **What does this access make possible, and what might it crowd out?**

In this guide, *human scale* means organizing tools and places around people's bodies, abilities, attention, time, relationships, and choices. A pocket-sized interface is one part of that picture. So are a safe crossing, an accessible doorway, a quiet room, and enough time to use them.

This is a working proposal. Its design suggestions and exercises have not been systematically evaluated. The public-health sources provide background; they do not establish that this particular guide will improve someone's health.

## 2. Keep information accessible

Making information harder to obtain in order to force people outside would create unnecessary barriers. A person might have a disability, limited transport, caring responsibilities, an unpredictable work schedule, or very little energy left in the day.

A person should be able to learn remotely, receive comfort, and rest. Walking should not become an entrance fee for information or dignity.

The aim is to make it easier to choose activities that meet a person's actual needs. Useful effort might involve practising a skill, making something, taking a walk, or working through a disagreement. A confusing website or a needless journey adds a different kind of effort, with no necessary benefit.

Remote relationships and participation can be meaningful in their own right. Physical attendance should be available and welcoming without becoming a test of whether someone belongs.

## 3. What the health evidence supports

WHO recommends that adults limit sedentary time and replace some of it with physical activity. Even light activity can provide health benefits. Its guidance includes people with disabilities and chronic conditions, with activity considered in relation to individual circumstances and abilities. [1]

WHO also recognizes social isolation and loneliness as public-health concerns with many contributing factors. Its discussion includes health, income, life changes, marginalization, community resources, and harmful digital experiences. Parks, libraries, transport, and local groups are among the community supports it identifies. [2][3]

These findings support opportunities for movement and connection. They do not establish that walking will resolve every mental-health difficulty or that every hour on a phone has the same effect. Reading, making art, contacting someone, working, and scrolling through upsetting material are different activities.

A guide to daily habits also cannot remove poverty, discrimination, unsafe streets, or inadequate services. Those conditions require action beyond an individual's screen choices.

## 4. Match the means to the need

Before asking whether someone should leave the screen, identify what they are trying to obtain.

| Need or purpose | What a phone can contribute | What may still be needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand a question | An explanation, sources, or a conversation | Checking the answer or applying it to a real example |
| Use a library or workshop | Opening hours, accessibility information, equipment availability | The materials, equipment, or shared space, if the task requires them |
| Keep in touch | A message, call, or shared online activity | Whatever form and frequency of contact the people involved want |
| Move and explore | Directions, an accessible route, or an invitation | Movement suited to the person and a safe opportunity to do it |
| Make something | References, planning, feedback, or digital tools | The practice and materials appropriate to the work, which may be digital or physical |
| Rest or find comfort | Music, a familiar voice, a book, or practical help | Permission to stop and the conditions that make rest possible |

Some needs can be met entirely through digital activity. Others can be supported by it. The person should have a meaningful choice about the combination.

## 5. Three places to improve the design

### Information that helps people act

Useful local information should explain when a place is open, what it offers, what it costs, and whether it is accessible. People should be able to check whether a trip is worthwhile before making it.

An AI assistant giving local information should distinguish verified details from assumptions and identify when the information was checked. A confidently invented opening time can waste someone's limited energy or leave them stranded.

Keep essential information available through accessible websites, telephone services, printed notices, or staff assistance where appropriate. Owning a smartphone or installing a particular app should not become the only practical way to participate.

### Digital tasks with a stopping point

A completed answer, saved plan, finished drawing, or arranged meeting can mark the end of a task. A humane service should make that completion easy to recognize.

Possible design choices include optional reminders, manageable notifications, clear save and export controls, and the ability to decline recommendations. An assistant can offer a concise checkpoint so a person can leave without losing their place.

The user should control whether another step is useful. A completed task should not automatically become a stream of new obligations or invitations to keep engaging.

These are proposed design choices. Their effects should be tested with users rather than assumed from an increase or decrease in screen time alone.

### Places people can reach and use

Safe crossings, maintained paths, accessible entrances, benches, toilets, shade, shelter, and public transport can make a destination more usable. Low-cost activities and places where no purchase is expected can make participation more feasible.

A neighborhood needs opportunities for both company and solitude. Someone may want a shared worktable; another person may want a quiet corner or a short visit without conversation.

Driving can be necessary for some people and circumstances. Walkable surroundings and accessible transport should expand the available choices. A trip should not require a car merely because the route or service was designed without alternatives.

## 6. A community hub example

Consider a hypothetical library or neighborhood hub with printers, shared tools, a quiet area, and occasional group activities.

A person checks their phone and learns that a printer is available, the accessible entrance is open, and a drawing group begins that afternoon. They save their file and choose whether to visit. The information helps them plan; the destination offers something they want to use.

At the hub, one visitor prints a worksheet and leaves. Another stays to draw. Someone asks for help with a file. A fourth person joins part of an activity remotely. Participation does not require a public performance, an account on a social platform, or a purchase.

Someone who cannot travel can still receive information and use the available remote services. The hub can explain which activities require physical equipment and what alternatives are available.

Success would mean that people can accomplish worthwhile tasks and participate on workable terms. Neither maximum attendance nor maximum online engagement would, by itself, establish success.

## 7. An optional practice: question, encounter, reflection, return

This practice connects inquiry with experience. It can be adapted to walking, wheelchair travel, a conversation, a creative task, or an observation from home.

1. **Question.** Identify one thing you want to understand. Use a book, another person, or AI to help clarify it.
2. **Encounter.** Try something, observe a place or object, or speak with someone willing to participate. Let the experience supply information you did not already have.
3. **Reflection.** Notice what supported the explanation, what contradicted it, and what remains uncertain.
4. **Return.** If useful, bring a short account back to the conversation or your notes. Revise the question or the work. Stop when you have enough for the present task.

For example, a person asks how to make a poster readable. They then look at a printed draft from different distances and notice that a subtitle disappears. On returning to the draft, they enlarge the subtitle and check it again. The physical test contributes evidence that the earlier discussion alone could not supply.

There is no required distance, duration, productivity target, or public post. An experience can remain private. It can also be complete without returning to a screen.

## 8. A small worksheet for everyday use

Choose one activity that you actually care about. This is an optional reflection, not a health score or a requirement to justify rest.

```text
What am I trying to obtain or understand?

Can the digital task meet that need by itself?

Would movement, materials, a place, or another person add something useful?

What barriers matter today: access, cost, safety, time, energy, or something else?

What would count as enough for this task?

What is one workable next step, including stopping or resting?

Afterward: what helped, and what would I change?
```

The answer might be to finish online, go outside, use public transport, contact someone, arrange assistance, or leave the task for another day. The exercise should support a person's judgment rather than prescribe one correct way to spend a day.

## 9. Questions for providers and communities

When reviewing an AI service, an app, or a community project, ask:

- Can a person complete the intended task and leave easily?
- Can they preserve their work without maintaining constant engagement?
- Does the service support accessible alternatives for people who cannot use its preferred interface?
- Does local information help someone judge whether a visit is practical and worthwhile?
- Are physical spaces affordable, accessible, and welcoming to different levels of social participation?
- Are claims about well-being supported by appropriate evidence?
- Are participants free to decline tracking, reminders, or public sharing that is unnecessary for the activity?

Useful evaluation would examine whether people achieved their goals, encountered fewer barriers, and retained control over their time. Any collection of personal feedback should be proportionate and voluntary. Health outcomes require appropriate research; they should not be inferred merely from attendance or engagement figures.
