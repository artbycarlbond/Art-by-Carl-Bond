## Use Note

This file is a compressed working guide.
It is meant to be quick,
digestible,
and usable on its own.
Treat its terms as working handles,
not gospel.

It is not a forecast that any specific job,
company,
tool,
or labor market will change in a particular way.
Those are empirical or current-use claims and need current sources.
This file is a standing and burden test:
when a productivity claim is made,
the reader asks who controls the tool,
who receives the gain,
who absorbs the cost,
what record proves the benefit,
and what capacity remains.

Work and automation practice is a workbench practice for testing claims about AI, productivity, labor, time savings, replacement, burden reduction, automated decisions, and agents.

Use it when a tool, manager, vendor, dashboard, policy, or AI system says work will become faster, cheaper, easier, safer, or more efficient. The practice asks one question in usable form: what cost moved, who now carries it, and what capacity remains after use?

Read "cost" broadly:
time,
money,
pace,
risk,
monitoring,
privacy,
retraining,
lost discretion,
appeal burden,
correction work,
and loss of skill all count.

This file does not ask the reader to defend every existing job shape. It does not treat technology as betrayal. Good tools can reduce drudgery, widen access, improve safety, support disabled workers, preserve records, and make hard work less punishing. The test is whether those gains return to the people who live with the tool.

## Worth Before Work

A person's worth does not come from an employer's demand for labor,
a market price,
or an owner's use for a task.

A tool may reduce the labor needed for production.
The person keeps a claim to livelihood,
time,
security,
participation,
and dignity.

Work can organize a person’s days,
relationships,
and sense of purpose.
When a role changes,
give people time,
material support,
and a voice in what follows.
Make room to continue,
learn,
teach,
change direction,
or rest.
A person does not have to become exceptional
at something else
to retain worth.

When a system says a person is no longer needed,
ask:
needed for what,
and by whom?

Trace the gain.
Does it return as shorter work,
safer work,
shared income,
care,
public capacity,
or greater freedom?
If owners keep the gain while workers and communities absorb lost livelihood,
productive capacity has become private insulation and public abandonment.

Lower production cost
does not by itself establish wider access.
Check who can now afford or use the result,
who remains excluded,
and who controls the terms.

Test a claim of returned public capacity
by naming its host and doorway.
Use `practice/gathering-anchors.md`
for one civic form.

When a tool reduces paid labor,
do not conclude that work has ended.
Care,
maintenance,
cleanup,
judgment,
and repair may remain
even when a market no longer pays for a task.

Trace what "spent" or "saved" means.
Was the money or gain consumed,
transferred,
converted into another asset,
retained under substantially the same control,
or recaptured later?
Who controls the next use,
and what capacity returned?

AI may help compare these paths,
or it may obscure them.
Test the claim.
It does not receive authority to define value,
decide whose needs count,
or determine how resources should move.

## Proportion And The Floor

Use proportion when a work or progress claim separates gain from burden.

Name the floor each affected person needs:
livelihood,
care,
time,
safety,
access,
participation,
and a real path to contest or refuse.
Different bodies,
places,
and care duties may require different support.

Then ask:

- How much relief returns to workers and communities?
- How much insulation accumulates with owners?
- Who absorbs loss if the project fails?
- Does private ambition depend on degraded public capacity,
  forced urgency,
  or lost livelihood elsewhere?

Treat balance as a repair process.
A stable arrangement can still be unjust.
In a repairable workplace or project,
people can see harm,
decision-makers face consequence,
and workers can force correction before burden cascades.

## Use When

Use this practice when a work claim feels too smooth.

Examples:

- "AI will save every worker five hours a week."
- "This system reduces administrative burden."
- "Automation will improve productivity."
- "The agent can handle routine customer contact."
- "The dashboard proves staffing is efficient."
- "This tool lets us do more with less."

Run the practice before adopting a workflow, approving a pilot, trusting a savings claim, replacing a role, automating a decision, or asking workers to adapt to a change they did not help shape.

Use [Know Yourself And Know The Person](../methods/ai-contact.md#know-yourself-and-know-the-person)
to choose workable forms of labor with the person.
If the proposal removes a physical mode,
apply its removal questions before counting the saving as a benefit.
Include the cost of keeping necessary access usable.

Use it for one claim first. Do not try to settle the future of work in one sitting. Name the claim, return it to the Five Returns, and leave with one next action.

## The Claim Being Tested

Write the work claim in one sentence.

Name who is making it, what work will change, who is expected to change behavior, what benefit is promised, and what proof is offered.

A clean claim should answer:

- What task, role, decision, contact, or workflow will change?
- Who says the change saves time, money, effort, risk, or burden?
- Who gets the saved time or money?
- Who must absorb retraining, monitoring, lost hours, changed pace, or correction work?
- Who can inspect, contest, slow, refuse, or repair the change?

If the claim cannot name the work being changed, treat the claim as incomplete.

## Work And Automation As Five Returns

**Body** asks what living workers, customers, patients, students, caregivers, managers, or communities must carry. Fear of job loss is not irrational; it is the body recognizing risk. Ask about fatigue, pace, injury, commute, surveillance stress, retraining time, emotional labor, hunger, sleep, childcare, and the strain of being told to keep up.

**Record** asks what evidence can answer back. Name the baseline, pilot data, job-impact notice, training record, audit log, error rate, appeal record, data source, decision trail, incident record, and before/after comparison. A productivity claim without a record becomes pressure.

**Consequence** asks who gains relief and who receives new burden. Ask who saves time, who loses hours, who becomes easier to replace, who must correct tool errors, who gets monitored, who loses discretion, who captures savings, and who carries the risk when the system fails.

**Commons** asks who has standing. Workers who live with the tool should have standing in its terms. Customers, patients, students, residents, and the public may also need a way to question, refuse, inspect, contest, or repair the result.

**Capacity** asks what the tool leaves behind. Does it preserve skill, method, local knowledge, mentorship, memory, access, safety, bargaining power, exit, and repair? Or does it leave dependency, deskilling, silence, faster pace, hidden cost, and no way back?

## Time Saved Is Not Enough

A time-saving claim needs a return path.

Use speed to remove needless burden. Preserve time for evidence, consent, judgment, and repair. Return saved time to the people who carried the work.

A humane schedule is part of that return.
It gives work a limit
and protects time for care,
rest,
and ordinary life.

Ask whose time is saved. Ask whose work gets compressed. Ask whether the saved time becomes rest, care, safer pace, learning, service quality, shorter shifts, better staffing, or shared gain. Then ask whether the saved time becomes layoffs, surveillance, higher quotas, lower pay, more customers per worker, or correction work that no dashboard counts.

Automation can reduce drudgery. It can remove repetitive typing, dangerous inspection, needless form work, duplicate entry, scheduling friction, translation barriers, or inaccessible interfaces. Those gains matter.

The claim remains incomplete until the reader can name who receives the relief.

A system that saves ten hours for management by adding ten hidden hours to frontline workers has moved the cost. A tool that removes one repetitive task while making the worker correct unreviewable errors has changed the burden, not ended it.

## Replacement, Standing, And Contest

Replacement claims require standing before reassurance.

A worker does not need to prove moral worth before asking what will happen to a job, schedule, wage, route, role, craft, or career path. Adaptation imposed after decisions are made elsewhere is extraction, not participation.

A workplace introducing automation should be able to answer:

- What work is changing?
- What jobs, hours, skills, or decisions are at risk?
- What data is being used?
- What decisions will the system suggest or make?
- What workers can contest?
- What retraining is funded and paid?
- What savings are captured?
- What gains are shared?
- What harms are measured?
- What protections remain after deployment?

Notice, contestability, retraining, gain-sharing, audits, and worker leverage turn adaptation into negotiation. Without those, productivity language can hide job risk, correction burden, surveillance, and loss of standing.

## Agents And Automated Decisions

Use this section when an AI system can act, contact people, change records, schedule work, evaluate workers, route benefits, approve or deny access, trigger discipline, send messages, or keep running after the user leaves.

AI may prepare action; it must not secretly become the actor.

A work agent needs narrow authority:
named task,
time limit,
resource budget,
human owner,
logs,
review and renewal points,
maintenance path,
stop path,
rollback path,
retirement condition,
and clear limits on data,
contact,
publication,
money,
employment,
and reputation.

This is an authorization standard,
not a claim about machine intention.
A system may be able to act,
but tool access,
human contact,
private data,
money,
employment,
public records,
and reputation require scope,
record,
review,
and a human owner before action.

Drafting is not sending. Suggesting is not deciding. Sorting is not denying. Flagging is not discipline. A system that touches employment, pay, benefits, scheduling, safety, housing, healthcare, education, public records, or legal standing needs stronger confirmation and human review.

If the system cannot show what it did, who it affected, what source it used, and how harm can be corrected, it should not govern the result.

## Drill: Ten-Minute Work Claim Audit

Set a ten-minute timer. Choose one work or automation claim.

1. Name the claim in one sentence.
2. Body: write what workers or affected people must feel, carry, learn, endure, risk, notice, or change.
3. Record: write what baseline, source, log, pilot result, job-impact notice, or audit would verify the claim.
4. Consequence: write who saves time, who loses time, who gains money, who carries risk, and who repairs failure.
5. Commons: write who can question, refuse, inspect, contest, appeal, bargain, or repair the change.
6. Capacity: write what skill, method, local knowledge, safety, access, exit, or dependency the tool leaves behind.
7. Choose one next action: verify, ask workers, request records, limit a pilot, require review, contest, revise, refuse, or hand off.

End with one sentence: what must happen before this claim guides work?

## Test Questions

- What work changes, and who named the need?
- What cost moved?
- Who now carries the cost?
- What record proves the promised saving or burden reduction?
- Who can contest the tool, the data, the decision, or the pace?
- What skill or method stops being practiced?
  A preserved record alone does not keep people able to perform it.
  For skills people need or choose to keep,
  who can teach them,
  and where will others have the time,
  tools,
  and opportunities to practice and pass them on?
- What gain returns to workers or affected people?
- What happens if the tool fails, disappears, changes price, changes terms, or blocks access?
- Can a person continue, appeal, or leave without losing livelihood, standing, memory, or safety?
  Who maintains the alternative route,
  what resources keep it usable,
  and when did people who rely on it last check it in use?
  When is the next check?

## Stop Conditions

Stop if the claim hides job risk, wage risk, schedule risk, safety risk, surveillance, or correction burden.

Stop if workers must adapt without notice, records, paid training, contest path, or leverage.

Stop if an automated system makes or triggers high-stakes decisions without human review, audit logs, appeal, and repair.

Stop if the tool needs private data, persistent access, external action, or worker monitoring beyond the stated task.

Stop if the proposal treats reduced headcount as proof of success without naming what happens to the people, skills, care, and local capacity attached to the work.

Stop if the situation requires labor law, contract review, safety review, union process, benefits advice, medical judgment, legal judgment, or public policy. Move to a qualified person or accountable process.

A humane work tool should reduce needless toil and return capacity. It should not make workers absorb all risk while someone else collects the upside.
