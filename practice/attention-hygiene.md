## Use Note

This file is a compressed working guide.
It is meant to be quick,
digestible,
and usable on its own.
Treat its terms as working handles,
not gospel.

It is not a diagnosis of a person,
a ban on screens,
or proof that any one attention practice works for everyone.
It is a formation test:
does the screen use return attention,
record,
body,
consequence,
commons,
and capacity,
or does it make the next unscreened moment harder to survive?

Attention hygiene is a workbench practice for returning screen-assisted thought to body, record, consequence, commons, and capacity. Use it to examine whether a screen, AI answer, feed, dashboard, prompt, or shared display is helping or displacing the contact and work the situation needs.

Judge a particular use by its purpose, content, design, setting, available alternatives, and effects on the people involved. State the strongest case for using it and the strongest objection. Give each claim the weight its evidence supports, and name what would change the judgment. Novelty, pleasure, unfamiliar taste, and ease do not establish harm. Learning, access, creation, connection, rest, and chosen entertainment can each be a reason to use a screen.

## Use When

Use this practice when you notice drift around a screen or AI tool.

That may look like a polished answer that keeps growing, a dashboard that feels more real than the people it describes, a feed that fills every pause, a meeting screen that captures the room, or an AI exchange that leaves you ready to speak before you have cooled, checked, or listened.

Use it with work, school, care, civic life, art, research, and conflict. Use it alone or with a group. Keep the frame practical: one screen-assisted abstraction, one return check, one clearer next action.

Choose changes with the person. Account for pain, fatigue, mobility, reading needs, work demands, and available support. A wheelchair user may need an accessible route or assistance outdoors; a reader with dyslexia may find [audio or text-to-speech](https://dyslexiaida.org/accommodations-for-students-with-dyslexia/) more accessible. Play can be seated, indoors, outdoors, or digital. Make room for recess, rest, and play, with opportunities to vary activity, attention, and surroundings that fit the person's needs and circumstances. Keep screen support needed for access or communication.

Keep necessary physical contact available as well.
A voluntary screen break cannot substitute for access
to paper,
objects,
places,
or people that someone needs.
Use [Know Yourself And Know The Person](../methods/ai-contact.md#know-yourself-and-know-the-person)
to distinguish a chosen pause from a requirement others must preserve.

Do not call the screen a bridge unless another side is actually reachable. When human support is unavailable, preserve proportion and a path back to body, record, place, solitude, or independent action without shaming the support the screen presently provides.

## Attention Economy As A Naming Handle

Use “attention economy” here as a naming handle for cases where a platform owner, employer, institution, or host converts human attention into metrics, influence, compliance, or private gain. Use the term to name the question. The archive is not an oracle, a rescue program, or an established path.

The literary warning sources in `source-shelf/perception-sources/canon-warning-vocabulary.md` supply questions and vocabulary. They do not verify current economic effects.

Keep each claim open until a person can point to a concrete record. Record time spent, interruptions, money or access conditions, notification and ranking incentives, work quotas, the ability to pause or leave, the cost of refusal, who receives the gain, and what capacity remains afterward. Repeat the same check over time before calling the pattern pacification or dependence. The size, growth, and hard economic impact of an attention economy remain unknown here and unverified by the archive.

## A Gentle Off-Ramp

At dinner, you might read something aloud from your phone and talk about it
together. If you keep missing what someone says while you scroll, you could save
your place, put the phone aside, and ask them to continue. Listening or sitting
in companionable silence counts.

A pause can be small and voluntary: part of a meal, a short walk, or time before
sleep. Keep any screen support you need for access or communication. You can
agree on time together without inspecting one another's devices. You do not
have to finish an AI conversation before leaving it.

An AI exchange may offer comfort or enjoyment. It must not replace human
interaction or be treated as coequal with it. Preserve the rest and human
relationships you want. A small return might be a shared meal or task through
`practice/gathering-anchors.md`, time alone, or sleep. Choose something within
reach, while keeping the support you need for access or communication.

## Drill: Ten-Minute Screen Return

Set a ten-minute timer. Choose one screen-assisted abstraction: an AI answer, prompt, feed habit, dashboard, meeting screen, classroom display, notification pattern, or public claim seen through a screen.

1. Name the abstraction in one sentence.
2. Body: write what it asks living people to feel, notice, carry, endure, wait for, or change.
3. Record: write the source, evidence, log, prompt, before and after state, or missing check.
4. Consequence: write who gets helped, burdened, exposed, distracted, excluded, or made responsible.
5. Commons: write who can question, refuse, inspect, correct, contest, or repair the screen use.
6. Capacity: write whether people leave more able to act, verify, remember, refuse, repair, rest, or decide.
7. Choose one next action: verify, wait, ask, revise, refuse, act, rest, repair, or hand off.

If the abstraction involves another person, pause the AI exchange before contact while keeping communication support available. If the issue involves a group, name who records the next action and when the group returns to it.

End with one sentence: what must happen outside the chat or away from the screen?
Check the [stop conditions](#stop-conditions) before continuing the exercise.

## The Rule

For this practice,
the default is to set aside unrelated feeds and background motion during the first act of making,
whether writing,
deciding,
planning,
learning,
caring,
meeting,
civic work,
or creative work.

The first act can be small: a sentence on paper, a spoken guess, a marked source, a sketch, a walk, a question to the person affected, a check of the room, or a rough list of what is known and unknown.

Screens may assist that work. They may help translate, record, search, calculate, remember, draft, compare, or connect. A source, lesson, or human exchange reached through a screen can be first contact. Check whether the tool helps someone engage with it or avoid it.

Necessary access is not passive drift.
Translation,
disability support,
memory aid,
notes,
magnification,
rest,
communication,
or chosen entertainment may be the humane first support.
Ask what the support makes possible, what it displaces, and who can choose, question, or end its use.

Look for specific patterns: notifications repeatedly interrupting chosen work, autoplay continuing beyond a chosen stopping point, a display crowding out conversation, or fluent answers replacing source checks. Record what happened and what else might explain it. Repeated difficulty stopping deserves review; enjoyment or long use alone does not establish dependence.

The screen may keep changing while the person's day remains still.
Ask what moved beyond it:
body,
task,
skill,
relationship,
record,
or place.

## Attention Hygiene As Five Returns

**Body** asks what the screen asks living people to do, feel, carry, notice, endure, wait through, or change. Start with appetite, fatigue, posture, visual comfort, breath, attention, sensory load, pain, rest, and time. Notice whether the person has comfortable, available ways to vary a task or position. If the body needs care, do not turn the audit into a performance of discipline.

Close work with books or screens is associated with childhood myopia (nearsightedness); genes also matter. [National Eye Institute (2017)](https://www.nei.nih.gov/research-and-training/research-news/myopia-close-look-efforts-turn-back-growing-problem). Myopia may need correction with [spectacles or contact lenses](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/nearsightedness-myopia). More outdoor time may reduce its onset; evidence for slowing existing myopia is less certain. [Cochrane (2024)](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013549_what-are-benefits-and-risks-interventions-increase-time-spent-outdoors-preventing-incidence-and). This differs from [age-related presbyopia](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/presbyopia), a common reason for reading glasses.

**Record** asks what must be written, checked, saved, sourced, compared, or corrected. A useful record can be small: the claim, the prompt, the source, the before and after state, the time spent, the next owner, or the line that needs verification.

**Consequence** asks who gains help, who takes burden, who loses attention, who gets exposed, who must repair the result, and what happens if the screen output is wrong. Consequence also asks whether the next unscreened moment gets easier or harder.

**Commons** asks who can question, refuse, inspect, correct, contest, or repair the screen use. In a classroom, meeting, office, gallery, clinic, or public space, shared attention belongs to more than the person holding the device or running the display.

**Capacity** asks whether people leave more able to act, verify, remember, refuse, repair, rest, or decide. If the tool makes the person smoother but less able to answer for the result, capacity has not returned.

## Necessary Friction, Not Purity

Necessary friction means preserving enough contact for attention and judgment to work. A person may need to wait, reread, try a first sentence, make a guess, ask after effort, sit with awkwardness, or notice the room before a tool answers.

Friction should help a person return. It should not punish exhaustion, disability, caregiving, grief, poverty, language barriers, or legitimate need for support. A short screen break may be humane. A reminder may protect memory. Translation may open the room. Notes may make participation possible.

Use friction as a check, not a badge. Ask whether the support lowers threat while leaving the next step with the person or group.

## Cognitive Offloading Discipline

Keep authorship, waiting, and responsibility with the person.

Calendars, notes, calculators, drafts, maps, checklists, reminders, search, and AI can lower strain. The danger begins when the tool takes over seeing, choosing, remembering, verifying, or meaning-making.

Before asking AI to smooth the work, make a rough human trace. Three plain sentences are enough for many adult tasks. For a child, a learner, or a tired person, the trace may be a drawing, a marked word, a spoken attempt, one step shown by hand, a question after rereading, or a list of what feels unclear.

Let a learner practice the same task with AI and without it when both routes are accessible.

After AI gives a clean answer, slow the handoff before carrying it into another person. Name the problem in one sentence. Mark what is verified. Mark what is feeling, fear, timing, or interpretation. Ask what you need from the other person and what can wait.

Pause the AI exchange before contact when the result carries conflict, care, accusation, apology, desire, grief, trust, or civic duty. Choose a pause that suits the person: rest, listen, change activity or position if comfortable, or revisit the source in an accessible format. Keep needed communication aids.

For an extended AI conversation, the return may need a deliberate hiatus. An open question can rest beside ordinary life while a person prepares food, washes a dish, finishes another task, or gives the answer time to lose its first authority. Solitude can protect the human core when the person leaves uncertainty open and does not turn reflection into reassurance, applause, identity, or dependence.

A hiatus can last a moment or an hour. Look away from the screen and witness something before turning it into a record, content, or spectacle. Speed does not turn every experience into value. The race between the hare and the tortoise does not prove that slowness always wins. It shows that speed alone does not determine the outcome. With patient, methodical attention, a person can stay with the work long enough to notice, correct, and finish.

After the AI exchange, ask: who did the waiting, kept the question open, bore the uncertainty, and remained responsible for what followed? If the human did, they may continue the exchange. If the person used the screen to avoid responsibility or noticed that the screen use had created drift, they may need to reduce AI use for a set period.

## Learning How To Learn, Throughout Life

Learning how to learn belongs to everyone,
throughout life:
elementary school,
high school,
trade school,
college,
and beyond.
Practice it at home,
at work,
in care,
repair,
craft,
relationships,
communities,
and public institutions.
People need opportunities to ask,
information they can inspect,
and room to disagree or correct mistakes.

“Why?” can organize inquiry.
Start with “What is happening?”
or “Can you show me?” if that is clearer.

Imagination opens possibilities: “What if we tried something different?”
Checking sources tests claims: “How do we know?”
Ask “Why does this matter, and to whom?”
and “Who benefits?” to examine consequences, interests, and power.
Ask “What else could explain this?”
and “What would change our minds?”
Allow the answers to change your conclusion.

There is no required order.
Sometimes observe,
listen,
or try something before asking further.
Let “why?” lead to a check,
a conversation,
an experiment,
or a clearer next step.

This everyday exercise draws on scientific inquiry:
consider alternative explanations,
test them against evidence,
and invite others to examine the reasoning.
Science uses multiple methods;
no sequence guarantees correct conclusions
or honest conduct.
Testing,
scrutiny,
and correction help make knowledge more reliable. [National Academies (2025)](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27894/chapter/4).

Choose one thing you need or want to understand.

1. Say what you know and what you need to find out.
2. Observe or try one manageable step. Name what you expect to happen.
3. Ask someone with relevant experience and check a source that can answer the question.
4. Explain the idea in your own words or show it in use. Credit what you learned from others.
5. Compare the result with your expectation. Correct the explanation or try a different approach.
6. Return later: what can you remember, apply, question, or teach, and what still needs help?

Words,
drawings,
demonstration,
notes,
communication aids,
and other accommodations can carry the work.
AI may help prepare a question or compare explanations.
It must not replace learning with and from other people.
Keep judgment with the learner and those affected.
The task should leave a next step,
not a demand to display certainty or perform understanding.

Creativity and critical thinking can be practiced and developed. [OECD's work with schools](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/fostering-students-creativity-and-critical-thinking_62212c37-en.html) offers teaching approaches and examples; it does not establish how well this archive works for independent readers.

The archive aims to help people carry inquiry into everyday life.
A meaningful result would be using these habits in another situation,
with the support the person needs,
without the archive or AI prompting each step.
Readers' experiences are needed to judge where the practice helps,
fails,
or needs correction.

The earlier human-only learning review remains an untested deployment idea,
not a requirement or a proven teaching method.
Any proposed assessment needs separate review of consent,
access,
privacy,
fair treatment,
and the right to challenge it.

## Shared Attention Infrastructure

A classroom board, meeting screen, public projector, teaching wall, or common display is shared attention infrastructure. Treat it as part of the commons.

Use `practice/gathering-anchors.md` when people need a shared subject or task so attention can rest somewhere without forced disclosure.

Do not fill shared attention space with unrelated feeds, background motion, autoplay, or stimulation that keeps eyes hooked while pretending to help. If instruction or discussion feels hard, reduce the burden with clearer segments, visible structure, pause points, paper, roles, breaks, drawing, standing, or direct examples.

Some exceptions belong: a break, a media-literacy example, a specific accommodation, a brief transition with a stop point, or a measured experiment with consent, purpose, and review.

The test is whether the support returns attention to the shared work.

## Test Questions

- Did the screen assist contact or replace it?
- Did someone make a rough human trace before the tool smoothed the work?
- Did the result return to body, record, consequence, commons, and capacity?
- Did shared attention remain available to the people in the room?
- Did the screen make the next ordinary act—dressing, eating, leaving,
  studying, working, caring, repairing, or resting—easier or harder?
- Did the person leave with a clearer next action?
- Did the practice protect access, accommodation, rest, and care?

## Stop Conditions

Stop the practice if it turns into shame, purity theater, or private self-control theater.

Stop if a person needs rest, food, medical help, disability support, translation, childcare, wages, transit, housing, safety, or care more than another audit.

Stop if the group uses attention language to police someone without giving them standing, access, or a repair path.

Stop if the questions delay a reversible next action that someone can take with care.

Stop if the issue is high-stakes, irreversible, legal, medical, financial, or harmful if guessed. Move to a qualified person, safer channel, source check, or accountable process.

Attention hygiene should return people to life and responsibility. It cannot replace rest, care, wages, policy, or repair.
