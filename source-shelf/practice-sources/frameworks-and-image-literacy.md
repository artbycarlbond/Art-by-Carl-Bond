The older frameworks source begins by lowering the gate.

It does not reject "prompt engineering" where that phrase names real craft,
but it resists turning image work into semantic mystique:
more words,
rarer terms,
or expert phrasing do not automatically produce a deeper image.

The preserved discipline is prompt literacy before prompt mystique.
A person chooses key constraints,
sequences the field,
looks at the result,
revises by seeing,
and stops adding language when the image needs clearer judgment instead.

The proof happens with the image on the desk or screen:
two drafts side by side,
one visible defect named,
one constraint changed,
and the next pass judged by eye.

Sometimes the next move is:

- a simpler phrase
- a stronger constraint
- a reference image
- an image-to-image pass
- a rebuild from visible evidence

The file's practical standard is accessibility:
a framework should help a beginner,
student,
artist,
worker,
teacher,
or neighbor make a better image without pretending access requires a priesthood
of special words.

## Public Mnemonics

The older source treats a public mnemonic as useful only when it survives
ordinary use.

A framework should be clear enough for a community board,
classroom wall,
library handout,
workshop sheet,
or notebook margin.

It passes the public-board test when:

- each part points to a real decision
- a beginner can use it without private explanation
- an expert can still respect the structure
- it helps action,
  not only memory
- the user can revise from evidence
  instead of reciting the acronym

Pre-work mnemonics were held as small handles,
not a stack:

- `S.M.A.R.T.` for vague goals:
  Specific,
  Measurable,
  Actionable,
  Reasonable,
  Timely.
  
- `S.C.O.P.E.` for scattered work:
  Subject,
  Context,
  Output,
  Parameters,
  Exit.
  
- `R.A.F.T.` for shaping a response:
  Role,
  Audience,
  Format,
  Task.
  
- `C.L.E.A.R.` for checking a claim before acting:
  Claim,
  Limits,
  Evidence,
  Alternatives,
  Responsibility.
  
- `S.T.O.P.` for rushed exchanges:
  Slow down,
  Type the claim,
  Observe assumptions,
  Proceed with the smallest responsible step.
  
- `P.A.C.E.` for preventing overreach:
  Purpose,
  Available capacity,
  Constraint,
  Exit.

The mnemonic sets the table.
The image framework does the deeper structuring.
Revision decides whether the result is worth carrying forward.

## Core Image Frameworks

### Reference Update — 30 September 2026

The names, fields, use cues, and fifteen teaching examples below match the revised public landing page.
They are exercises for organizing an intention, inspecting a result, and revising it.
Use words or short phrases; the field labels are cues, not requirements for a single correct image.

Use the acronym to find descriptive terms.
The image generator creates the visual language.
Inspect the result and revise as needed.

The expanded examples on the landing page use photorealism and square proportions.
These choices are yours to keep or change:

- Style or medium: [your choice]
- Image proportions: [your choice]

The sequences here are current teaching examples.
Earlier demonstration images may carry earlier wording.
A revised caption does not establish which prompt originally generated an image.

### F.O.C.U.S. — Building Core Structure

Use when you want to establish the subject, setting, arrangement, distinctive detail, and mood.

**Fields, in order:**

- **Focal Subject (Noun):** What the image is primarily about
- **Overall Setting (Noun):** Where the image takes place at the widest scale
- **Composition Layout (Descriptor):** How the scene is arranged spatially
- **Unique Detail (Noun):** What gives the image a memorable anchor
- **Sensory Tone (Descriptor):** What emotional or atmospheric tone the image should carry

**Teaching examples:**

- **Baseline — serene nature:** Mountain → Highlands → Layered → Waterfall → Majestic
- **Contrast — harsh desert:** Cactus → Dune → Isolated → Mirage → Harsh
- **Contrast — urban street:** Vendor → Market → Crowded → Lantern → Vibrant

### V.I.S.I.O.N. — Bringing Interaction and Narrative

Use when the image depends on what subjects are doing, how they relate, and what their surroundings suggest.

**Fields, in order:**

- **Visual (Descriptor):** How clear, soft, sharp, or visually filtered the scene should feel
- **Illumination (Noun):** What kind of light governs the scene
- **Subject (Noun):** What the image centers on
- **Interaction (Verb):** What is happening between elements
- **Opacity (Descriptor):** How clear, hazy, dense, or transparent the atmosphere feels
- **Narrative (Abstract Noun):** What deeper story or meaning the image should suggest

**Teaching examples:**

- **Baseline — majestic clarity:** Natural → Backlight → Peak → Descending → Misty → Persistence
- **Contrast — urban tension:** Obscured → Neon → Figure → Fleeing → Hazy → Survival
- **Contrast — ethereal intimacy:** Soft → Candlelight → Figures → Embracing → Translucent → Memory

### D.A.L.L.E. — Adding Finer Details

Use when you want to specify materials, surface details, movement, lighting, and environment.

**Fields, in order:**

- **Details (Descriptor):** What fine-grained qualities the image should emphasize
- **Actor (Noun):** Who or what is carrying the action
- **Locomotion (Action):** What movement, activity, or process is occurring
- **Light (Descriptor):** What quality of light shapes the scene
- **Environment (Noun):** Where the action is taking place

**Teaching examples:**

- **Baseline — timeless nature:** Frosted → Mountain → Flowing → Soft → Arctic
- **Contrast — mechanical detail:** Polished → Machine → Grinding → Harsh → Foundry
- **Contrast — organic intricacy:** Delicate → Flower → Blooming → Sunlit → Meadow

### P.A.N.E.L.S. — Refining Depth and Atmosphere

Use when viewpoint, spatial arrangement, lighting, and atmosphere are central to the image.

**Fields, in order:**

- **Position (Viewpoint):** From where the scene is viewed
- **Ambience (Descriptor):** What mood the scene carries
- **Need (Abstract Noun):** What emotional or sensory state the image reaches toward
- **Environment (Noun):** Where the image takes place
- **Lighting (Descriptor):** What quality of light shapes the scene
- **Structure (Descriptor):** How the image is organized compositionally

**Teaching examples:**

- **Baseline — serene mountains:** Aerial → Serene → Ascension → Highlands → Dappled → Layered
- **Contrast — grassland softness:** Ground-level → Verdant → Renewal → Meadow → Diffused → Open
- **Contrast — tranquil shoreline:** Eye-level → Calm → Reflection → Coastline → Golden → Smooth

### S.A.M.O.S.E.T. — Quick Conceptual Ignition

Use when you want to explore a scene through its subject, action, surrounding elements, tone, setting, era/time, and theme.

**Fields, in order:**

- **Subject (Noun):** What the image centers on
- **Action (Verb):** What the subject is doing
- **Matter (Noun):** What additional element complicates or enriches the scene
- **Overall (Descriptor):** What overall tone the image should carry
- **Setting (Noun):** Where the scene takes place
- **Era/time (Temporal descriptors):** The scene’s historical period, time of day, or both
- **Theme (Abstract Noun):** What deeper idea the scene should express

**Teaching examples:**

- **Baseline — majestic peak:** Mountain → Rising → Clouds → Majestic → Highlands → Ancient → Resilience
- **Contrast — fleeing human:** Figure → Running → Rain → Restless → Street → Modern → Longing
- **Contrast — still life:** Flame → Flickering → Shadows → Fragile → Room → Victorian → Hope

### Recomposition Protocol — Current Practice

Use when an image is close to your intention but still needs work.
Supply the image, describe what to keep or change, and include the original prompt if available.

Ask for a concise description of what is visible, with uncertain details identified as uncertain.
Then ask for up to three changes that support your stated intention, including what each might sacrifice.
Keep the description and suggestions separate.

If the original prompt is supplied, revise it.
Otherwise, write a new prompt from the visible image and your stated intention.
Preserve the subject, setting, and qualities you want to retain unless your request calls for changes.
Choose realism, natural light, sharper detail, or another style according to your intended result.
Review the proposed prompt before generating or editing an image.

Describe visual qualities directly: viewpoint, brushwork, shapes, palette, contrast, and texture.
The exercise avoids camera brands, artist names, celebrities, fictional characters, and franchise names as shortcuts.

The point is not loyalty to a formula.
The point is a better image,
clearer judgment,
and more usable revision.

The old file also sets a limit on framework sprawl:
one pre-work mnemonic,
one primary image framework,
and a second framework only when the visible image asks for refinement.
If the workflow needs more than that,
the likely problem is not a lack of frameworks.
The concept,
constraint,
or visible evidence probably needs to be clarified.

## Limits

A framework cannot:

- guarantee obedience
- rescue a weak concept
- replace taste
- replace revision
- force realism from contradiction
- remove the need to compare outputs

Frameworks help structure intent.
They do not remove the burden of judgment.

If a framework stops helping,
simplify,
switch frameworks,
or return to the visible evidence.

## Operational Pressure

These review practices follow the revised landing-page epilogue.
They guide the participant's inspection and choices:

1. **Start With the Main Intention.** State the subject, action, purpose, and essential details; check whether the result reflects them.
2. **Describe What You Want.** State desired elements and relevant exclusions; consult the chosen tool's instructions and inspect both.
3. **Specificity Defines the Request.** Specify details that matter and leave variation where you welcome it.
4. **Structure Helps You Check.** Use lists or labeled fields to inspect missing details and conflicting instructions.
5. **Use the First Pass for Orientation.** Compare the result with your intention and retain versions you may want to revisit.
6. **Refine With a Clear Purpose.** Name the intended change and what should stay; inspect the whole revision for unintended changes.
7. **Choose Settings for the Intended Use.** Check available settings, dimensions, and stated time or cost; inspect the result at its intended size.
8. **Check Every Rendered Word.** Compare spelling, punctuation, order, missing or added text, and legibility with the requested wording.
9. **Check Features That Must Stay Consistent.** Compare important details in logos, icons, faces, and repeated motifs after edits.
10. **More Words ≠ More Control.** Give each detail a purpose; remove unhelpful repetition and resolve conflicting instructions.
11. **Position Within the Frameworks.** Use the fields to organize choices and clarify conflicts in relation to your intended result.
12. **Final Orientation.** Compare, inspect, question, and decide what to keep, revise, or stop. Keep the final decision with the participant.

## Visual Ethics Boundary

Image generation is not consequence-free play when identity,
sexuality,
politics,
medicine,
law,
reputation,
or public trust are involved.

Do not use framework language to create deceptive identity evidence,
imply false endorsement,
fabricate event documentation,
launder public-trust claims,
or make the image feel more authoritative than the record permits.

When an image wants belief,
return to the active truth and claim standard.
