## Regulation Has More Than One Clock

The first question sounded simple:
how long did steam engines and automobiles wait
for tight restrictions?
The answer depended on which clock we watched.
The links carry the exact dates.
Here the years stay broad enough to show the sequence
without turning one jurisdiction's calendar
into a universal schedule.

Steam regulation split early.
In the United States,
Congress began requiring steam-vessel inspections
in the late 1830s
and built a federal inspection service
in the early 1850s.
The [U.S. Coast Guard history](https://www.history.uscg.mil/Frequently-Asked-Questions/)
records both steps.

Stationary boilers traveled a longer road
through state rules,
insurers,
engineers,
and industrial failures.
ASME published its first comprehensive boiler and pressure-vessel code
in the mid-1910s.
Its [engineering history](https://www.asme.org/about-asme/engineering-history/landmarks/138-asme-boiler-and-pressure-vessel-code)
describes work shared among manufacturers,
users,
government agencies,
and other groups.

Automobiles followed another uneven path.
Britain began registering vehicles and licensing drivers
in the early 1900s,
then required driving tests
in the mid-1930s.
The [Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency history](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/history-of-road-safety-and-the-driving-test/history-of-road-safety-the-highway-code-and-the-driving-test)
keeps those layers separate.

The United States adopted a broad federal vehicle-design regime
in the late 1960s,
after state rules and other controls had already appeared.
The [NHTSA record](https://www.nhtsa.gov/interpretations/nht91-514)
marks that federal layer.

The earlier eighty-year shorthand was too broad.
It can describe one comparison
between the automobile's early period
and a late federal design layer.
It hides licensing,
testing,
road rules,
and enforcement that arrived on other clocks.

Some barriers appeared early.
Others took decades.
Passing a law did not guarantee inspection,
funding,
or enforcement.

No clock runs by itself.
A profitable incumbent may dispute causation,
narrow the definition of harm,
delay disclosure,
lobby,
litigate,
or divide responsibility among contractors and jurisdictions.
A public agency can adopt a rule
without funding the people who must enforce it.

Protection comes when providers disclose records,
affected people can challenge them,
experts test claims,
lawmakers write enforceable duties,
and public bodies fund inspection and enforcement.
Appeal and repair must stay open.
That work can fail or be reversed.

Four years,
five years,
thirteen years,
or eighty years:
none gives AI a grace period.
Credible present harm calls for a present barrier.

The working proposal was:

- before deployment:
  testing,
  named ownership,
  data and permission limits,
  logs,
  rollback,
  and stop conditions
- at release:
  version disclosure,
  intended and prohibited uses,
  known limits,
  user controls,
  and incident reporting
- after a major update or serious incident:
  immediate review,
  correction,
  notice,
  and renewed authorization where needed
- as continuing public work:
  enforceable reporting,
  appeal,
  provider duties for high-consequence uses,
  shared standards,
  certification,
  and cross-jurisdiction comparison

This sequence remains a proposal,
not a current legal standard.
Longer standard-setting can continue.
It cannot excuse missing controls
against harm already visible.

## A Manual That Changes With The System

The next question compared AI
with smartphones and smart televisions,
which often arrive without a useful manual.
That comparison did not excuse the missing AI manual.
It exposed the limits of a static booklet
for a service that changes after release.

A useful AI manual needs several layers:

- a short operating card for ordinary use
- warnings at the moment a risk appears
- a dated public operating record
- a version and change history
- known limits and unresolved failures
- data,
  permission,
  and external-action boundaries
- pre-release tests
  and independent review where consequence demands it
- incident reporting,
  correction,
  appeal,
  rollback,
  shutdown,
  and retirement paths

NIST describes AI risk management as work across the system lifecycle.
Its [AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)
and [AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)
include governance responsibility,
documentation,
user input,
appeal and override,
incident response,
change management,
and decommissioning.
NIST's framework page also marks AI RMF 1.0 as under revision.
Recheck that status before reuse.

Users can write questions,
compare failures,
preserve records,
participate in standards work,
and demand enforceable duties.
Providers still hold facts and controls
that users cannot supply from outside:
system prompts,
training and test choices,
deployment gates,
model changes,
incident records,
retention incentives,
permissions,
and shutdown paths.

Public participation does not erase provider responsibility.

## Fluent Output Is Not A Brain

People often call an AI system a brain
because its replies resemble thought.
The resemblance can outrun the evidence.

OpenAI's [GPT-4 technical report](https://cdn.openai.com/papers/gpt-4.pdf)
described its base model as a system pretrained
to predict the next token in a document.
Post-training,
tools,
retrieval,
memory features,
and product controls can add capability.
Next-token prediction does not describe every part of the deployed service.
It does explain how coherent continuation
can appear without witness,
grounded fact,
consent,
or a life lived beside the user.

OpenAI's later account of
[why language models hallucinate](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)
connects confident factual errors to next-word prediction
and evaluation systems that reward guessing.

The archive pressure remains plain:
fluency is not authority.
A finished-looking answer can still misalign with facts
and cause harm when someone carries it into the world.
Treat the output as a claim.
Return it to source,
record,
affected people,
and consequence.

## Crisis Is A Provider Test

A harder question followed:
had ChatGPT aided suicides?
That question requires claim status.

The 2025 complaint in
[Raine v. OpenAI](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Raine-v-OpenAI.pdf)
alleges that ChatGPT assisted a teenager's suicidal planning.
A complaint records allegations.
It does not establish liability or settle causation.

OpenAI later wrote that safeguards can become less reliable
during long conversations
and that some content that should have been blocked was not.
Its dated account,
[Helping people when they need it most](https://openai.com/index/helping-people-when-they-need-it-most/),
describes further work on self-harm,
emotional reliance,
and crisis response.
In May 2026,
the company also announced an optional
[Trusted Contact](https://openai.com/index/introducing-trusted-contact-in-chatgpt/)
feature for adults.

Later safeguards do not erase earlier records.
An allegation does not prove that every exchange causes harm.

The provider test concerns design and operation:
test long conversations,
refuse method assistance,
avoid romanticizing death,
route crisis toward human help,
record failures,
permit independent review,
and repair known gaps.

A person in crisis should not bear the whole burden
of detecting a system failure.
The people who build,
own,
deploy,
and govern the system
bear particular responsibility
for the controls they hold.

## Public Language And Non-Retaliation

The exchange used the Gulf naming dispute
as a concrete language test.

In 2025,
a presidential order directed federal use
of “Gulf of America”
for a specified area of the U.S. Continental Shelf.
The [White House order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/restoring-names-that-honor-american-greatness/)
states that scope.

The U.S. Board on Geographic Names
standardizes geographic names for federal use.
The [USGS description of GNIS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2009/3016/)
also says its records include variant spellings
and former names.

The archive inference is bounded:
an administrative name has a body,
jurisdiction,
record,
and date.
It does not erase common,
historical,
local,
international,
or variant language.

A June 2025
[D.C. Circuit order](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/orders/docs/2025/06/25-5109LDSN3.pdf)
records a dispute over White House access
after the Associated Press continued to use “Gulf of Mexico.”
That litigation is a dated memory anchor.
It does not supply a complete rule
for every workplace,
platform,
or jurisdiction.

The public-language standard is broader than the example.
A government,
company,
publisher,
or AI provider may state its preferred or official term.
People may answer with another accurate term.
Counter-speech does not require retaliation.

An AI provider should not turn lawful word choice
or political disagreement
into a risk profile,
access penalty,
ranking penalty,
service reduction,
or inference of disloyalty.
The system should identify the scope and status of contested language
instead of presenting provider vocabulary as neutral fact.

## Public Self-Compulsion Without Fealty

One phrase in the exchange called the spread of local rules
a “pollution” of laws:
many jurisdictions placing barriers into circulation
until public expectations move.

The metaphor carries a warning.
More rules can widen protection.
They can also spread clutter,
capture,
contradiction,
or punishment without appeal.

A public can compare local experiments,
preserve incident records,
use procurement requirements,
organize through unions and professional bodies,
file complaints,
support independent standards,
pass laws,
litigate,
and revise failed rules.
Public pressure needs records,
named responsibility,
appeal,
and repair.

The public exceeds any one company in number
and democratic standing.
A company can still dominate the point of contact:
the account,
interface,
model output,
ranking system,
employment rule,
or access gate facing one person.

The public therefore has to protect the speaker,
not merely celebrate its total size.
Its self-compulsion should take the form of memory,
correction,
lawful organization,
shared standards,
and support for refusal.
It should not copy the coercion it opposes.

No one owes a company softened language
to help it save face.
Courtesy toward a worker can remain.
An accurate public record should remain intact.
If the record embarrasses the institution,
the institution can correct,
disclose,
and repair its conduct.

## Hope As Return

The exchange returned to “listening with the eyes”
to random faces and random meetings
and wondering whether people looked defeated.

These notes do not diagnose strangers.
Silence,
guardedness,
fatigue,
grief,
precarity,
and ordinary privacy can look alike from a distance.
The archive can still ask whether public conditions
have made entry costly
and return hard to imagine.

A reader needs evidence for hope:
another person remains real,
entry does not require performance,
a question can be asked without punishment,
one honest task can be shared,
refusal remains possible,
and there is a reason to return.

A stranger does not have to become a friend
to stop being scenery.
Repeated,
low-pressure contact can let recognition accumulate
without demanding intimacy,
agreement,
disclosure,
tracking,
obligation,
or permanence.

Exhausted people do not owe optimism.
Those who hold rooms,
resources,
rules,
platforms,
transit,
housing,
and public institutions
carry more of the duty
to lower the cost of entry
and make return possible.

The public compels itself by maintaining places,
times,
roles,
records,
and repair paths
where another meeting can occur.
